## TL;DR

The hardest campaigns to improve are the ones that run intermittently: budget tight this month, paused; remembered next month, resumed. The cost isn't just missed traffic — it's that the data can never answer the only question that matters. Fix continuity first. Efficiency second.

## Six months of delivery, and nothing knowable

A campaign opens, runs when budget allows, pauses when it doesn't. Half a year passes and you genuinely cannot say how it performs: this stretch caught a sale, that stretch lasted a week, three weeks in the middle went dark.

Is it any good? **For a stop-and-go campaign, that question has no answer** — and the reason is worth more attention than the wasted traffic.

## The three layers of damage

**Layer one: the missed traffic.** The obvious one — paused weeks have no delivery; intermittent advertising buys at a discount in every sense.

**Layer two: perpetual cold starts.** Every restart re-runs learning and re-earns placement. A campaign that toggles constantly spends a large share of its budget re-heating instead of delivering.

**Layer three: your own judgment goes blind.** The one that matters. In continuous delivery, week-over-week comparisons mean something — "this week worse than last" is information. In intermittent delivery, every stretch has its own context: **bad numbers might mean weak delivery, or they might mean the on-off pattern broke it; good numbers might mean a strong campaign, or a lucky seasonal tailwind.** Nothing is decidable, because nothing is comparable — so no optimization can even begin.

## Self-check: delivery density

One ratio: **delivery density = weeks with real spend ÷ total weeks in the span.**

- High density (running most weeks): the data is readable; efficiency judgments are licensed
- Low density (days on, days off): fix continuity first — efficiency questions wait

For a discontinuous underperformer, the order of operations:

1. **Restore continuous delivery** — one uninterrupted stretch, funded properly
2. **Accumulate comparable data** — at least two to three settled weeks (the ledger itself takes ~16 days to close: [Why marketplace ad data needs 16 days before you judge it](/blog/1688-p4p-ad-data-16-day-settlement))
3. **Then run the verdict** — only now does "it performs badly" become a trustworthy conclusion, and the stop decision follow the normal discipline ([Five Checks Before You Kill an Underperforming B2B Ad Campaign](/blog/1688-campaign-stop-checklist))

Killing the discontinuous campaign outright is the mirror-image error — executing a campaign that was never actually tested, and feeling decisive about it.

## When budget genuinely can't cover everything

Intermittency is usually budget pressure wearing a disguise. If the money can't keep every campaign running, the answer is not "run all of them at half-throttle with gaps" — it's **contraction**: concentrate budget on the few campaigns you can fund continuously, and formally pause the rest with a record (evaluation rules for eventual restarts: [Should You Restart a Paused B2B Ad Campaign?](/blog/1688-paused-campaign-restart)).

Five campaigns running continuously beat fifteen campaigns all "intermittently open" — the former produce data that guides the next step; the latter produce fifteen noise generators.

<InfoBox variant="warning" title="One sentence to remember">

Stop-and-go delivery makes data permanently unreadable: check delivery density, restore continuity before judging anything, and when budget runs short, shrink the front — never let every campaign run intermittently.

</InfoBox>

## FAQ

### What's wrong with running a marketplace ad campaign on and off?

The data loses comparability: each gap changes the context, so you can't distinguish 'weak delivery' from 'the interruptions broke it' — and without that, no optimization is even possible.

### How do I know if a campaign is stop-and-go?

Delivery density: weeks with real spend divided by total weeks in the span. Low density means restore continuous delivery before judging anything about efficiency.

### A stop-and-go campaign performs badly — what's the first move?

Not stopping it — restoring continuity. After a continuous stretch produces comparable data, a still-bad verdict is finally trustworthy, and the stop decision can follow properly.
