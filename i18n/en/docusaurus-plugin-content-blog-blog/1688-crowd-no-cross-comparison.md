## TL;DR

Sorting audiences by cost-per-inquiry and cutting from the bottom is the most intuitive move in audience management — and the most destructive. The numbers are not comparable across audiences; the "worst" ones you cut are often the least expensive mistake and the most expensive one at the same time.

## You ranked, you cut, and new customers stopped coming

The monthly instinct: sort the table, demote the expensive half, promote the cheap half. Clean spreadsheet hygiene.

Two months in, new-customer flow thins. Revenue starts leaning on repeat buyers. Look back at the cut list: those "expensive" audiences were the ones introducing strangers to your store. The spreadsheet was right about the numbers and wrong about the business.

## Reason one: audiences overlap, so the books don't balance

Marketplace audiences are not sealed boxes. The same buyer matches several audiences in the same week — one impression logs to audience A, one inquiry logs to audience B. Spend and inquiries leak across the borders constantly.

Each audience's reported cost is therefore mixed, not owned. Ranking mixed numbers against each other ranks contamination.

## Reason two: organic reach can't be subtracted

Pause an audience's paid delivery and it may still generate spend and inquiries through organic and overlapping exposure. That contribution can be attributed to neither side cleanly.

So every audience's reported figures are "paid plus an unknowable mix," and the mix ratio differs per audience. Comparing them is comparing mix ratios.

## Reason three: funnel position makes "expensive" meaningful

Prospecting audiences meet buyers who have never heard of you: recognize, compare, hesitate — the longest path to an inquiry, and short-term numbers that always look mediocre.

They are also where next quarter's customers come from. Cost-rank them and they sit at the bottom every single month; manage by that ranking and you drain the upstream supply that your downstream retargeting and repeat-purchase audiences quietly depend on. The damage surfaces two or three months later, by which time nobody connects it to the cut.

## The comparison that actually works

**Vertical, not horizontal.** Each audience against its own history: is this month better or worse than its own recent past, and by how much? Same definitions, same audience — that is a signal. The trend mechanics are in [The Three-Month Trend Rule for B2B Ad Bid Decisions](/blog/1688-crowd-premium-3-month-trend).

And when the whole store's numbers jump together — promotions, holidays — that is not something per-audience comparison should absorb at all. Freeze the month entirely; see [Don't touch B2B ad bids in abnormal months](/blog/1688-crowd-skip-abnormal-months).

<InfoBox variant="warning" title="One sentence to remember">

Read audiences vertically against their own trends, never horizontally against each other. The bottom of a cross-audience ranking is not "worst" — it is usually "upstream."

</InfoBox>

## FAQ

### Can I rank ad audiences by cost-per-inquiry and cut the worst?

No. Audiences overlap, organic traffic contaminates paid numbers, and funnel positions differ — the ranking measures contamination, not performance. Judge each audience against its own history.

### Why do prospecting audiences always look expensive?

They reach first-time buyers whose path to an inquiry is longest, so short-term numbers look flat. They feed the top of the funnel; cutting them hollows out the next quarter's customers.

### Is comparing against the store average better?

No. Each audience reacts to store-wide shocks differently, so one shared baseline amplifies bias. Store-wide anomalies need a freeze, not per-audience comparisons.
