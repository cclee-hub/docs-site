## TL;DR

Killing campaigns is where marketplace ad budgets leak fastest. Underperformers do deserve stopping — but "looks bad" and "is bad" are different things, and five checks separate them. Only the fifth fails fast; the first four all say "wait."

## The campaign you killed on Monday was innocent

Monday's report: decent spend, no attributed orders. You stop the campaign. Two weeks later a competitor's similar campaign takes off, and re-reading your own delivery log shows the campaign ran barely a week with a gap in the middle.

It wasn't performing badly. It hadn't yet performed at all.

A false kill costs twice: the inquiries the campaign would have produced, and the cold restart if you change your mind. "Stop" deserves triple the caution "start" gets.

## The five checks, in order

### Check one: has it run long enough?

At least one to two full settlement periods of actual delivery? Under that, no verdict is possible — this isn't patience, it's that the data cannot support a conclusion. New campaigns are owed an observation window. (And remember the ledger itself takes ~16 days to close: [Why marketplace ad data needs 16 days before you judge it](/blog/1688-p4p-ad-data-16-day-settlement).)

### Check two: has it spent enough?

A campaign spending pocket change per week completes no meaningful test. When spend is that thin, poor results usually mean "underfunded," not "broken." Raise to a level that generates data, then judge.

### Check three: has it run continuously?

Stop-and-go campaigns can't be evaluated: every gap changes the context, so you can't tell whether delivery is weak or the interruptions broke it. **Discontinuous and underperforming → the first move is restoring continuity**, not stopping. Only after a continuous stretch produces comparable data does the performance verdict mean anything. The full evaluation flow is in the [1688 P4P optimization method](/docs/1688-ad-optimization-guide).

### Check four: did it get a learning period?

A campaign just starting to produce its first inquiries deserves one more cycle. Kill it at first light and you never learn what the next step looked like. The operational meaning: while the sample is too thin, "stop" is simply not an available conclusion — lock it out until one full observation cycle completes.

### Check five: is it truly zero-inquiry — at scale?

All four passed, and the account still reads: **accumulated spend clearly beyond what a reasonable cost-per-inquiry would allow, zero inquiries.** This is the only immediate-stop signal. At that scale of burn with nothing coming back, the acquisition path never connected. Stop now; don't wait for next month.

## The table

| Check | Failing sign | Action |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| Runtime | Under ~a week of delivery | Wait |
| Spend | Too little to test anything | Fund it, then judge |
| Continuity | On-and-off delivery | Restore continuity first |
| Learning period | Killed at first signs of life | One more cycle |
| Zero-inquiry at scale | Burned past any reasonable CPI with zero | Stop immediately |

## FAQ

### How long should a B2B ad campaign run before judging it?

At least one to two full settlement cycles. Campaigns with short runtime or thin spend lack the data for any verdict — stopping them is usually a false kill.

### Which failure justifies stopping a campaign immediately?

Accumulated spend clearly above a reasonable cost-per-inquiry with zero inquiries to show for it. That is the one unambiguous stop signal — the acquisition path never started working.

### How do I judge a campaign that runs on and off?

Fix continuity first. Stop-and-go data cannot answer whether delivery is bad or the interruptions caused it — restore continuous delivery, then evaluate.
