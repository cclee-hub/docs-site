## TL;DR

Identical failing data on a fringe product and on your store staple should trigger different actions: the fringe product can be handled directly; the staple gets downgraded to observation first. Not double standards — different blast radii.

## The pause that dented the whole store

A promoted product turns in weak numbers; the removal recommendation looks justified; you execute.

Then store-wide revenue dips noticeably for two weeks. That product's share of store revenue was far bigger than its ads ledger suggested. The same data blemish that means "stop losing pocket money" on a fringe item means "stop the presses" on a staple.

**Sensitivity to false kills is proportional to the product's share of the store.** That is the entire logic of best-seller protection.

## Why staples earn the slower trigger

**1. The recommendation thresholds are tuned for ordinary products.** Keep-or-stop indicators (conversion, order trends) trip within normal fluctuation ranges. For a fringe product a false trip costs pocket change; for the store's top earner it costs the month. Same threshold, very different tolerance.

**2. A staple's value overflows its own ads ledger.** High-revenue products double as traffic front doors and trust anchors: buyers enter for the staple and leave with the catalog. An ads-ledger-only verdict misses that spillover entirely.

**3. Their dips are more often weather.** Market swings, seasonality, a competitor's promotion — external shocks land hardest on the biggest numbers because the base is big. One more observation cycle resolves most of these into clarity.

## Protection means "observe, then decide" — not "hands off"

The actual protocol:

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| Recommendation fires | Don't execute; downgrade to monitored observation |
| Observation window | Watch subsequent settled periods: does the dip clarify or deepen? |
| Still deteriorating | Pause anyway — protection doesn't renew forever |
| Numbers recover | Resume normal delivery; log the false alarm |

Two misconceptions to break:

- **Protection is not immunity.** Observation is not an open-ended stay: two more cycles of genuine worsening means the problem is real. Protection moves the decision from "today" to "once it's clear" — it never deletes it.
- **Protection cannot block campaign-level stops.** If the staple's entire campaign gets paused, product-level protection is moot — the campaign stop is an operational fact. Which is exactly why campaign-level decisions belong at the very top of the caution hierarchy: they silently override every product-level buffer beneath them.

## Identifying your staples

No analytics required: **would the store hurt within a week if this product's ads stopped?** If yes, it's a staple. For a more objective cut, check the revenue ranking monthly — anything contributing a meaningful double-digit share of store revenue gets staple treatment.

## FAQ

### Should a top-selling product's ads be paused the moment data turns bad?

No — downgrade to observation first. Staples carry high false-kill costs; one more cycle usually clarifies whether the dip is noise or real. Protection delays the call, it doesn't block it.

### Are best-sellers exempt from being paused?

No. If deterioration persists through the observation window, pause anyway — protection exists to prevent impulsive kills, not to grant immunity to failing delivery.

### When does best-seller protection not apply?

When the whole campaign is paused. Campaign-level stops are operational facts that product-level protection cannot block — which is why campaign-level decisions deserve the most caution of all.
